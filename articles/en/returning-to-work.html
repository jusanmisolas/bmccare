Returning to work and daily tasks

Print / Save as PDF

When can you return to work?

Returning to work after a heart event depends on your condition, treatment, recovery, and job. Lifting, driving, and working in heat may place different demands on you from desk work. Ask your doctor when you can return and what limits apply. Another person’s experience is not your medical clearance. [1,2,3]

Describe the job in detail

Tell the team what a usual workday involves. Include lifting, stairs, walking, long periods of standing, night shifts, stress, and the journey to work. Mention whether you operate a vehicle or machinery. Explain the tasks you cannot easily avoid. Describe specific tasks; a job title may not show the demands or safety responsibilities.

Ask about a staged return

Depending on your health and workplace, a gradual return or temporary change in duties may be worth discussing. Ask your doctor what is medically appropriate and your employer what is possible. Confirm any written recommendations you need. Do not promise a schedule before the care team has reviewed your readiness. A planned review can help you assess whether the arrangement remains suitable. [2,4]

Keep care in the schedule

Consider how medicines, meals, rehabilitation, and follow-up visits fit around work. Ask about conflicts before they lead to missed treatment. Keep necessary contact details available and plan transport for appointments. If you are self-employed or paid daily, explain that situation clearly. The team may not be able to solve the financial problem, but understanding it helps make the discussion more realistic.

Include unpaid responsibilities

Housework and caregiving can be demanding too. Ask about carrying groceries, lifting a child, cleaning, and other tasks you normally do. Returning home does not automatically mean you are ready for every responsibility. Discuss temporary help with the people involved. A written list of restrictions can make it easier to explain why some tasks need to be shared for now.

Speak about fear and pressure

You may feel eager to resume normal life, worried about symptoms, or pressured by lost income. Tell your care team rather than treating those concerns as private obstacles you must hide. Ask about emotional support if fear is affecting recovery. At work, share only the health information needed for the agreed arrangement. You can discuss limits without telling every colleague the details of your medical history.

Review rather than push through

Agree on a review date and whom to contact if work is too demanding. Do not push through new chest pressure, severe breathlessness, or fainting; seek urgent help. Ask for reassessment before increasing duties if your plan requires it.
