Understanding your blood pressure

Print / Save as PDF

What is blood pressure?

Blood pressure is the force of blood against artery walls. When it stays too high, it can damage blood vessels and increase the risk of heart, brain, and kidney problems. High blood pressure may cause no symptoms. Regular checks and your treatment plan help you manage it. [1,4]

Understand the two numbers

The upper number describes pressure when the heart beats. The lower number describes pressure between beats. Ask your doctor to explain your readings and set a target that fits your health. One measurement does not tell the whole story. Your team may consider repeated readings, medicines, other conditions, and how you feel before deciding whether treatment needs to change. [1,3]

Learn before checking at home

If home monitoring is recommended, ask which device and cuff size are appropriate. Bring the monitor to a visit if the team asks you to. Have someone demonstrate the position, rest period, and timing they want you to use. Write down the instructions. Avoid repeatedly checking out of fear without a plan, because extra numbers may add confusion rather than useful information. [3,5]

Keep an honest record

Record readings as instructed, with the date and time. Add symptoms or relevant notes rather than selecting only the numbers you like. Bring the record to your appointment. Ask what result needs a phone call and what requires urgent care. Do not change a medicine dose based only on a home reading unless your doctor has given you a specific written adjustment plan. [1,3]

Work on everyday habits

Treatment can include prescribed medicines and changes in food, activity, sleep, and other habits. A lower-sodium eating plan may help, and your team can suggest a safe level of movement. These changes support treatment rather than giving permission to stop medicines. Choose an achievable step and review it with your team. Tell them about barriers such as cost, pain, or an irregular work schedule. [2]

Make questions specific

Instead of asking only whether a reading is good, ask what it means for you. Is the target the same at home and in the clinic? When should you measure? What should you do if the device displays an error? What if you feel dizzy? A short list of questions can make the next visit more useful and leave you with fewer guesses.

Know when to get help

Ask your doctor for a clear action plan for very high readings. If you have chest pain, severe breathlessness, weakness, difficulty speaking, or another sudden serious symptom, seek emergency care immediately. Do not wait to collect several more readings or for the rehabilitation unit to answer an email. Keep emergency instructions where the people helping you can find them.

Prepare for the next visit

Bring your home-monitoring instructions and recent readings to follow-up. Discuss any result that concerned you and ask whether the timing or method of measurement needs to change. Confirm whom to contact with questions.
